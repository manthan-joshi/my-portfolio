The point of this project was to take a CHO cell line through a complete CRISPR editing and validation workflow, not just the flashy part with the scissors. We got through the whole workflow. Why this matters

CHO cells are the workhorse of biologics manufacturing, responsible for the majority of recombinant therapeutic proteins on the market. Being able to confidently edit them with CRISPR and verify the edit actually happened is foundational work for any biotech that wants to modify a production line, knock out a problematic gene, or introduce a new one.

HPRT1 is a classic selection marker locus. Edits at HPRT1 can be selected using 6-TG or HAT media, which made it the right choice for a project focused on methodology rather than therapeutic payload.

What we actually did

  • Designed the full experimental workflow for CRISPR/Cas9 editing at HPRT1, including guide selection and the validation strategy
  • Assembled Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes at roughly a 1:1 Cas9 to guide molar ratio and transfected adherent CHO cells using CRISPRmax
  • Built and ran 8-point, 3-fold serial kill curves in duplicate for both 6-TG and puromycin on a CellTiter-Glo readout, to set the selection window
  • Ran the downstream train: genomic DNA extraction, Phusion PCR across the edit site, agarose gel electrophoresis, amplicon clean-up and Nanodrop QC, then bidirectional Sanger sequencing and ICE
  • Ran the plasmid engineering side in parallel: bacterial transformation, colony screening, plasmid purification, and a NotI and EcoRV restriction digest
  • Managed the CHO culture throughout: seeding, serial passaging, cryopreservation and selection

Where it broke

Both kill curves came back flat. 6-TG relative viability sat between 96.2% and 103.1% across all eight concentrations, and puromycin only reached 74.3% at the top of the range. The entire dataset, both plates, spans 0.042 to 0.069 RLU, which is the real tell. Two unrelated cytotoxic agents failing identically inside one narrow signal band is an assay problem, not a biology problem.

With no dose response to read, the selection concentration was taken from the middle of the range rather than derived from the curve. Seven days later the no-transfection control was still alive.

Sanger returned unreadable traces and ICE failed at the control file, which blocks every sample in the batch, so no indel rate was ever produced. On the digest, NotI cut and EcoRV did not, leaving construct identity unconfirmed. Supercoiled DNA runs ahead of linear, so the upward band shift in the NotI lane is what a successful cut looks like, and reading that correctly took a second pass. The knock-in arm was lost to microbial contamination before it generated data.

Everyone can run a transfection. The value is in the downstream: what did you actually edit, and how confident are you that you edited it?

What I took from it

The biggest thing this project taught me was how much genome editing work is actually careful validation work. The edit is the easy part. Trusting that the edit happened, at the right place, with the expected frequency, is where real scientific discipline shows up.

Which is what happened next. I went back to the raw plate data and rebuilt the whole workflow as a process development report: unit operations and in-process controls, CQAs and CPPs, eight numbered deviations with CAPA, a qualification plan for the readout that failed us, and a screening design for doing the delivery step properly. That document is the better half of this project and it is linked below.

It is also the project I can talk through most fluently in an interview, for exactly that reason.
